How AI governance actually fails

Existing frameworks catalog attacks on AI systems and vulnerabilities in AI applications. AGFM catalogs the failures of the governance process itself — the use case that reached production without approval, the human review that exists in policy but not in the system, the approval nobody revisited after the model changed, the action item that has been open for seven months.

01

What AGFM is

The AI Governance Failure Mode framework (AGFM) is an open, community-maintained catalog of the ways enterprise AI governance processes fail. Each failure mode carries a permanent identifier, a plain-language example, the signals that indicate it is present, its root causes, the controls that address it, and a role-scoped, date-stamped mapping to the regulations and standards it implicates.

  • It is a shared vocabulary. AGF-F040 is something a risk officer can put in a finding, a ticket, or an audit response. "The human oversight problem" is not.
  • It is a detection aid. Every entry leads with observable indicators, so a team can determine whether a failure mode is present without hiring anyone to tell them.
  • It is a crosswalk that states its own limits. Every mapping names who the obligation falls on, when it applies, and how precisely it has been verified. Mappings that are aspirational are labelled as such rather than asserted.
  • It is written to be understood. Every entry opens with a concrete example in ordinary language, before any framework vocabulary appears.
Why plain language is a design rule, not a courtesy

AI governance is new work for most of the people now responsible for it — often a risk manager, a quality lead, or an operations director rather than a specialist. A catalog that only makes sense to people who already understand the problem cannot help the people who need it most. Every entry opens with "In plain terms": one concrete situation, no acronyms, no framework references. If a failure mode cannot be described that way, the entry is not finished.

What AGFM is not

  • Not a maturity score. There is no AGFM rating, level, or grade.
  • Not a certification. Conformance assessment, where it exists, is a separate published methodology carried out by accredited assessors.
  • Not software. A knowledge base, designed to be implemented inside whatever tooling an organization already uses.
  • Not legal advice. A mapping identifies which provision a failure mode plausibly implicates. It does not interpret that provision, determine applicability, or establish compliance.
  • Not a blame framework. Almost every failure here is the predictable output of a reasonable process under load, not of anyone behaving badly.

Identifier policy

Identifiers are permanent. Once assigned, an AGF-F number is never reused and never renumbered, even if the failure mode is later deprecated or merged. Deprecated entries remain published with a pointer to their successor, so citations in audit records and tooling do not break.

Why lifecycle stages organize the matrix

Regulation was rejected as the organizing axis because it moves too quickly. In the eighteen months to September 2026 alone: Colorado repealed its own AI Act before it ever took effect, the EU deferred its high-risk regime by sixteen months while leaving transparency duties in force, and US banking's model risk guidance was superseded after fifteen years. A structure keyed to regulation would need re-architecting annually. Roles were rejected because ownership of AI governance differs wildly between a regional bank, a cell-therapy manufacturer, and an industrial processor.

The governance lifecycle is the stable axis. Regulatory mappings therefore live as fields on each entry, where they can be versioned and date-stamped independently of the structure that holds them.

05

Cross-cutting patterns

Individual failure modes are useful. The relationships between them are more useful, because they explain why fixing one thing in isolation usually fails.

1. Friction causes evasion

The most reliable cause of ungoverned AI is governance that is too slow to use. When every request receives the same heavyweight review regardless of risk (AGF-F027), when no turnaround commitment exists (AGF-F037), and when the same question gets re-litigated from scratch every time (AGF-F038), teams stop submitting. The observable result is intake bypass (AGF-F010) and unsanctioned use (AGF-F011) — almost always treated as discipline problems when they are throughput problems. Enforcement without a fast path makes the measured problem worse while making the real problem invisible.

2. Reviews without remediation are theatre

A review that produces findings nobody tracks (AGF-F100), with no owner or due date (AGF-F101), no escalation when they age (AGF-F102), and no evidence required to close (AGF-F103), generates the appearance of governance and none of the effect. This is the most common gap between programmes that look mature and programmes that are.

3. Gate order determines whether gates work

When a review stage requires artifacts that a later stage produces (AGF-F039), every item routes through it, fails, and returns — permanently and structurally. Teams normalize the loop rather than reordering the gates. The symptom presents as slowness; the cause is sequencing, and no amount of additional resourcing fixes it.

4. Approvals are states, not events

Treating approval as a one-time decision rather than a time-boxed state produces stale approvals (AGF-F031), silent scope expansion (AGF-F032), unverified conditions (AGF-F033), and systems running on authority granted against a model that no longer exists (AGF-F071). All are invisible in a register that records only that approval occurred.

5. Governance records are themselves a system that can fail

Reorganizations rewrite historical attribution (AGF-F096). Rigid data models cannot record what actually happened (AGF-F097). Corrections have to be made by hand across hundreds of records (AGF-F098). Configuration differs between environments so records quietly lose fields (AGF-F075). An organization can operate every control correctly and still be unable to demonstrate it, because the evidence layer degraded underneath.

Practical implication. A remediation plan that addresses failure modes one at a time, in identifier order, will underperform. Fix the throughput problem before enforcing the bypass problem, build the remediation loop before adding review depth, and reorder gates before adding reviewers.

Regulatory crosswalk

The crosswalk is the most useful and the fastest-decaying part of this catalog. It is therefore governed by explicit conventions rather than presented as settled fact.

How to read a mapping

Every mapping in every entry carries three things beyond the citation itself:

ElementWhat it tells youValues
Role scopeWho the obligation actually falls on. This matters more than any other element and is the most common source of error in published crosswalks. Most organizations using AGFM are deployers — they buy and operate AI systems. A large share of EU AI Act obligations fall on providers — those who develop a system and place it on the market. Citing a provider obligation to a deployer produces a compliance plan for a duty they do not hold, while missing the one they do.Provider · Deployer · Provider & deployer · Organization · Banking organization
StatusWhether the obligation is currently enforceable, scheduled for a future date, or subject to a litigation stay. A deferred obligation is not an absent one, but it is not the same planning problem.In force · Future date · Stayed
PrecisionHow specifically the mapping has been verified. AGFM cites EU AI Act provisions at article and, where verified, paragraph level; ISO/IEC 42001 at clause level; NIST AI RMF at function and category level.See the note below

On NIST subcategory precision

NIST AI RMF 1.0 contains 72 subcategories across 19 categories and 4 functions. AGFM currently maps at function and category level — for example GOVERN-1 rather than GOVERN-1.5. Subcategory-level mapping is a v1.0 target and will be verified against the NIST AI RMF Playbook before publication. Approximate precision stated honestly is more useful than false precision stated confidently, and a subcategory reference that turns out to be wrong damages the credibility of every other mapping alongside it.

Corrections issued in v0.3

Published crosswalks propagate errors. AGFM records its own, so that anyone who built on an earlier version can correct it:

  • EU AI Act post-market monitoring is Article 72, not Article 61. Serious incident reporting is Article 73. Article 61 concerns informed consent for testing in real-world conditions and was cited in error in v0.1 and v0.2 on entries AGF-F031, AGF-F080 and AGF-F100.
  • NIST AI RMF structure was misstated. Earlier versions described "GOVERN 6 categories / 19 subcategories." The correct structure is 4 functions, 19 categories, and 72 subcategories in total.
  • Colorado mappings were materially wrong. Earlier versions cited Colorado for anti-discrimination duties. SB 26-189 removed the algorithmic-discrimination duty and the impact-assessment mandate when it repealed SB 24-205. Colorado now maps to notice, explanation, human review and correction rights — not to bias testing. Bias-audit mappings now anchor on NYC Local Law 144.
  • SR 26-2 supersedes SR 21-8 as well as SR 11-7, and its generative and agentic carve-out sits specifically at footnote 3 to § II.
  • Role scoping was absent entirely. Every mapping now names whether the obligation falls on a provider, a deployer, or the organization generally. This was the largest structural defect in earlier versions.

Mappings are research aids, not legal advice. AGFM identifies which provisions a failure mode plausibly implicates. It does not interpret those provisions, determine applicability to any particular organization, or establish compliance. Consult qualified counsel for any of those questions.

De-identification rule

No published entry may contain an organization name, an individual's name, a product name, or any combination of sector, size, and circumstance that renders a contributor identifiable. This applies with particular force to plain-language examples: they are composites, written to be recognizable to many organizations and traceable to none.

08

Contributing

AGFM is built from observed practice. Contributions are welcome from anyone who governs AI systems in production — risk, audit, quality, model risk, data governance, and AI programme roles in particular.

Submit

Propose a failure mode

Describe a governance failure you have observed, generalized so that no organization or individual is identifiable. Submissions go through public editorial triage.

Improve

Complete an open entry

Sixty-seven enumerated modes await full schema. Indicators and mitigations from practitioners are worth more than any amount of desk research.

Correct

Challenge a mapping

Wrong article, wrong role scope, superseded instrument, missing jurisdiction. Mapping corrections are the highest-priority contribution class and ship on the next minor version with a named correction notice.

Translate

Sharpen a plain-language example

If an example reads as jargon or would confuse someone new to governance work, say so. Clarity contributions are treated as substantive, not cosmetic.

Editorial process

  1. Triage. Scope fit, de-identification, and duplication against existing entries.
  2. Drafting. Written to the standard schema, including the plain-language example and role-scoped mappings. Anonymous contribution is the default.
  3. Review. Entries touching regulatory mapping receive a second review against primary sources before publication. Secondary sources are not sufficient for a mapping.
  4. Release. Published on the next scheduled version with a changelog entry. Corrections to previously published mappings are recorded explicitly rather than edited silently.

Release cadence

Minor versions quarterly. Major versions annually. Regulatory monitoring runs continuously, and material changes to a cited instrument trigger an out-of-cycle correction notice rather than waiting for the next scheduled release.

Sponsorship disclosure

AGFM was founded and is currently underwritten by AlignAI, which provides secretariat support. AlignAI holds a minority voice on the advisory board and receives no editorial privilege. Sponsorship does not confer influence over which failure modes are catalogued or how mitigations are described, and no AGFM entry references any commercial product, including the sponsor's. We disclose this openly because a catalog of governance failures is worth exactly as much as its independence.

Citing AGFM

Cite individual failure modes by identifier and version. Identifiers are permanent and remain resolvable after deprecation.

In tooling
Reference the identifier alone — AGF-F040 — with the framework version recorded once at the tool or report level.
Cite the version with any mapping

Regulatory mappings change between versions, and four instruments cited here changed materially in the eighteen months to September 2026. A mapping quoted without its framework version cannot be checked against the instrument as it stood at the time. Always record the AGFM version alongside any mapping reproduced in a report, finding, or control document.

Version status

v0.3 is a public draft. The structure, identifier scheme, and schema are stable enough to build on. Identifiers assigned in v0.1 onward are permanent and carry forward.

Changes in v0.3: added role scoping to every mapping; corrected the EU AI Act post-market monitoring citation from Art. 61 to Art. 72/73; corrected the NIST AI RMF structural description; rewrote all Colorado mappings following the SB 24-205 repeal; added Art. 4, Art. 17, Art. 26 and the Art. 50 paragraph-level detail; recorded the SR 26-2 supersession of SR 21-8 and the footnote 3 scope carve-out; added a published corrections notice.
